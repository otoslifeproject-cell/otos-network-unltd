When ADHD is left waiting, addiction steps in.And it doesn’t come quietly.

The system usually treats addiction as the primary condition. For some people, that is right. For a significant and identifiable group of adults, it is not. The primary condition is ADHD. The drinking or drug use is what arrives in view.

The problem

The system treats the crisis. Not always the condition driving it.

I am what this problem costs. Six detoxes, two ICU admissions, a suicide attempt, mental health teams, courts, probation, benefits, housing, addiction services and a funded residential rehab — every one paid for by the system, none of them aimed at the condition driving it. My ADHD diagnosis and my waiting medication review were documented throughout — and not ignored, because ignored would mean somebody weighed them. They were never considered. No pathway requires anyone to look for the ADHD sitting under an addiction; connecting the two is no one’s role in today’s system. That is what has to change — and it is what the demonstrator exists to evidence. Even NHS England’s own independent ADHD Taskforce agrees — it has since described my life in a single paragraph:

“ADHD, when unsupported, is a potent route into educational failure, long-term unemployment, crime, substance misuse, suicide, mental and physical illness. Economic costs to individuals and the government of at least £17 billion are avoidable as, when appropriately supported, people with ADHD can thrive and fully engage in a working life.”

Report of the independent ADHD Taskforce, NHS England, 2025

People wait with unmanaged ADHD. Then they reappear — in A&E, in addiction services, in crisis teams, in detox, in rehab, in housing pressure, in benefits queues, in police contact, in mental-health crisis, in collapse and sometimes in prison. By the time the system catches up, the original need has become much more expensive and much harder to unwind.

£17bn

avoidable annual economic cost of unsupported ADHD in the UK

Independent ADHD Taskforce, NHS England, 2025

Self-medication works — until it doesn’t. For an untreated ADHD brain, alcohol can briefly deliver what the brain cannot make for itself: regulation. Focus, relief, calm, social access, a temporary correction of baseline. That is not a moral failure. It is a neurological system finding something that, for a time, helps it function. And then it crosses the line into dependence and compounds everything it once relieved.

Then the treatment itself misses. Recovery is a learning process — therapy, relapse prevention, recovery education and routine — and it asks for exactly the capacities unmanaged ADHD impairs: planning, working memory, impulse control and delayed reward. Fund recovery without stabilising the ADHD underneath it and you are asking someone to succeed using the one system that has been disabled.

If my ADHD had been recognised, treated and held properly earlier, I do not believe alcohol would have become the painful, life-changing, system-level problem it later became.

NHS England’s own ADHD data carries a warning about itself: it is “likely to significantly underrepresent the number of people seeking, waiting for and with a diagnosis of ADHD.”

The Royal College of Psychiatrists has been explicit about what should happen: “Significant and comorbid substance misuse should be managed jointly between general adult and addiction services in accordance with existing local protocols” (CR235 §11.1, verbatim). Its 2025 report on co-occurring conditions goes further — “assertive linkage to appropriate mental healthcare should be considered a core role for substance use services” (CR243). On the question every addiction service asks first, CR235 §11.1 is unambiguous: “when prescribed stimulants are used appropriately, there is little evidence of patients becoming dependent, and indeed treating ADHD may reduce the lifetime risk of developing a substance use disorder.”

Treat the addiction alone and some of us keep coming back. Treat the ADHD underneath it and the recovery work can finally hold.

The guidance exists. The connective infrastructure to deliver it does not.

02

The gap

Nobody owns the risk in the gap.

Every service owns its own episode. Nobody owns the time between them. It is not a gap in care — it is unowned risk.

The gap persists, year after year, because no one’s job is to fill it.

The hospital owns the admission. The addiction service owns the treatment episode. The ADHD service owns the assessment. The GP owns the consultation. No one is accountable for the days, weeks and months in between — and that unowned time is exactly where this group is lost.

A referral made, but nothing lands. A discharge happens, then silence. A signpost given, but no follow-through. Known to services is not the same as held between services.

They do not disengage loudly. They drift, silently, at hand-off — after a detox, after a discharge, maybe signposted, but alone at the moment of highest risk. A referral is not proof that a connection happened. A discharge plan is not proof that the next step landed. A waiting list is not a holding layer.

And there is a reason it happens to this group in particular. A handoff is not an administrative event — it is a cognitive task handed to the patient: remember something said once, hold it across weeks and start an action with no immediate reward, while unwell. For an unmedicated ADHD brain, that is the hardest possible ask. Reward is steeply discounted over time, so a date three weeks away never feels close. Working memory is leaky; the instruction was verbal. Initiation, planning, working memory, emotional regulation, delayed reward — a handoff quietly demands them all and unmanaged ADHD impairs every one. What recovery requires, ADHD has already taken away. Miss the first appointment and shame makes the second harder. Meanwhile the untreated condition keeps generating the state that alcohol reliably fixes.

Drift is not a choice. It is a predictable consequence of a known neurology — which means it can be designed for.

And when a service discharges, its role is properly finished and the case closes — by design. What nobody is holding is that the ADHD came before every one of those presentations and treatments, and it is still there, still waiting. It is the condition underneath everything the system keeps paying to treat — and today it is only ever met downstream, after the crisis, if at all. Dealt with upstream — surgically, as the special cases they are — the whole pattern changes: I personally believe recovery rates will increase and the massive cost chain that follows this cohort should collapse as a direct result.

This cohort is not hidden. It is in the room, being counted, being paid for — but invisible.

And yet the sector has begun to look. Published CGL material suggests prior development of an ADHD assessment pathway within a substance-use / criminal-justice context — in CGL’s own words, “the development and implementation of a specialist ADHD diagnostic assessment pathway in Nottinghamshire for adults using substances within the criminal justice system”. The sector has begun operationalising the question. CGL Nottingham is not OTOS proof. It is pathway proof. ADHD in addiction is not a private hunch. OTOS is not proposing diagnostic duplication; it is proposing continuity visibility between services so agreed next steps stop disappearing between silos — where they cost the system more money and, too often, lives.

03

The venture

Infrastructure, not another service.

OTOS Continuity™ is a shared Continuity Layer between the person and the services already around them — one shared Layer carrying many individual Threads across the services people already use. Each Thread is one person’s journey; the Layer holds them all at once, with authorised partner views and separate service records.

The intervention is clinical and belongs to clinicians — early ADHD diagnosis and correct medication, urgently. OTOS does not do the intervention. It is what makes the intervention reachable and what holds it afterwards. Everything it does runs in two directions at once:

  1. Inwards, to the person — micro check-ins of varying kinds through the day, chosen by the person with their keyworker: prompts, medication reminders, saying hello — and small engineered behaviour-reinforcement tasks that replicate the intensity and repetition of residential rehab work. “Do a three-minute meditation.” “Write down the four Cs of recovery and put the note on your fridge door.” The content belongs to the services — OTOS is the infrastructure that carries it. New neural pathways are only formed through repetition — twelve weeks minimum, which is exactly what the demonstrator gives them. Simple, small, constant — so the person is held through the day, when a keyworker’s reality is one call a fortnight.
  2. Outwards, to the services — every check-in is multi-purpose: a reply is a signal — he’s alive, engaged with the recovery. Even the delivery receipt is a signal — the phone is on. Signals create the alerts. OTOS surfaces the alerts — before crisis becomes consequence. Referrals recorded, delivery confirmed, attendance logged, handoffs receipted — and ADHD status updated, so every partner sees progress without asking. It works in every direction at once: a wellbeing hub sends its course invitations down the same thread, waiting-list support and short recovery content travel alongside them and the addiction service can see the person engaged with the course and still waiting for the review — without a single phone call. And if he turns up at A&E, that surfaces as an alert on the Layer: the person is in crisis and everyone who should know, knows. A referral is a signpost. A handoff receipt is a connection.
Services deliver to it and receive from it. The person receives from it and replies to it. Continuity does the holding both inwards and outwards.

A check-in is not surveillance, it is a heartbeat: the person is still there, so care continues quietly in the background. The alarm only matters because the silence is the thing that currently goes unseen. Consent-led signals throughout, so the whole thing can be evaluated properly — including being shown not to work.

Nobody owns the risk after discharge, the gap between appointments, the time alone. Continuity becomes a digital waiting room — not passive waiting, active holding — carrying the person through it. Generic reminders do not understand shame, avoidance, relapse, ADHD paralysis or the moment someone is close to disappearing.

The most important referral may be the very first one: a nurse at a hospital bedside, after a detox, registering the person on Continuity instead of saying “try the addiction service or maybe mental health.” From that one act the scene is set — the ADHD team knows immediately and can fast-track, the addiction service chases the person instead of waiting for them and the thread stops snapping at discharge. One referral at the point of crisis and the whole problem is being tackled.

Every existing connection between a service and the person continues underneath, unaffected.

Crucially, it needs no access to anyone’s clinical record. NHS England’s own framework on integrating voluntary-sector organisations names the barrier plainly: caution about breaching confidentiality or GDPR and no legal framework to share data between the NHS and VCSE organisations. A consented, non-clinical continuity layer sidesteps that entirely — which is why it can be tested in months rather than waiting on a national programme. We are not asking the system to change; we are not asking services to connect to each other. Each partner connects to Continuity, which creates the connection for both: log in and there is a connected view. Small, low-effort — and it evidences what the mounting research already points to and what nobody is currently looking for.

What OTOS is

  • A shared Continuity Layer between the person and the services already around them — non-clinical, always
  • Consent-led, with the person in control of their own thread
  • Grounded in NICE NG87 and RCPsych CR235 / CR243 guidance

  • Designed to be tested and to be stopped if it does not work

  • A general messaging platform for existing activity
04

The founder

I am not adjacent to this problem. I lived it.

I was diagnosed with ADHD in 2020. I was referred for an NHS medication review in March 2021 — and I am still on that waiting list. Five years and counting.

For twenty-five years, drinking worked really well for me. I didn’t realise it, but it was the medication my undiagnosed ADHD required every day to simply function. Then the drinking stopped working but didn’t stop being necessary — and started costing: six detoxes, two ICU admissions, a suicide attempt, A&E, courts, probation and residential rehab at a dual-diagnosis hospital — where my ADHD medication was removed on arrival because it was a stimulant. I came out of that rehab with over a thousand hours of therapy in my body — thirteen hours a day, twelve-stepped, addiction-informed at a level most people never reach. And it held — for two years. I forgot about the ADHD. The ADHD did not forget about me. Then, with no medication underneath and no self-medication left either, I was living untreated ADHD raw for the first time in my life and it slowly eroded everything the recovery work had built. Two years on recovery work alone — I never felt right and at the time I accepted that discomfort as sobriety. Then a nine-month controlled-drinking experiment, run with a design: if I got into trouble, pull the emergency cord — A&E, detox — and I would know for sure. It re-ran my whole story in miniature: the drinking briefly restored the function, I built on it, then it crossed the line — in months instead of decades. It ended in a one-week relapse that properly gripped me and in asking my parents for help: it has to be the ADHD. Every service I met did its own job properly. The system saw the alcohol, the crisis, the admissions and the collapse. It did not connect the documented ADHD underneath quickly enough to change the trajectory. The thing that nearly killed me wasn’t being out of a service. It was being between services and nobody knowing.

In September 2025 I reached the right medication privately, funded by my parents. I felt the change within hours. The recovery work that had floated for years finally landed — not because the addiction work hadn’t been good, it had been excellent, but because I finally had a brain underneath that could use the hard work. And in April 2026 an independent consultant psychiatrist wrote what nobody had connected: “adult ADHD, previously complicated with excess alcohol use to self-medicate inadequately managed ADHD symptoms.”

I did not recover by waiting my turn. I recovered by navigating a fragmented system fast enough to reach the intervention that changed my life. My story isn’t unique. My survival is.

ADHD is a disability under the Equality Act 2010. I am not an applicant who happens to have relevant lived experience — I am building the thing I needed and could not get, for the people who cannot do what I did. Many of them do not yet know they have ADHD at all. They are simply living the pattern I lived.

That experience is not the pitch. It is the design specification. I was invited to join the APPIE network — the NIHR Policy Research Unit in Addictions’ national lived-experience research network — their approach, not mine.

The award

What the money does.

Twelve months, three stages and a specific thing that exists at the end of each. This award does not build the product — it builds the case that makes the product fundable.

WhenWhat gets built
Months 1–3The local evidence base: the local picture assembled and the research question defined.
Months 4–6The governance pack that makes a service able to say yes: data protection impact assessment, consent model, safeguarding protocol, information-governance review and clinical safety documentation developed with Clinical Safety Officer review.
Months 7–12Insurance, partner agreements — and a costed, specified demonstrator that a commissioner or funder can be asked to fund.

This is a trial to find out whether what happened to me is happening to others already visible to services here in Cambridgeshire and Peterborough — and without reconsideration and intervention it will continue to do so, costing more money and more lives.

What we will measure — reported at commissioner grade: handoffs receipted and next steps reached · re-engagement time after a missed contact · micro check-in delivery, replies and engagement across the education and wellbeing content · ADHD pathway progress, referral to review to prescription · what participants say about being held — and, asked openly: do we add burden for the practitioners involved? Small. Surgical. Enough to prove or stop.

06

Sustainability

Three routes and one of them is already open.

1 · Local-authority and NHS commissioning — live and moving

A cost-redirection opportunity hiding in current drug and alcohol spend. A public health commissioner in Cambridgeshire asked to see the full proposal following a meeting in April 2026 — that proposal is now being delivered, together with the offer of a first, small piece of commissioned analysis. And the ground has shifted in OTOS’s favour: the county runs an active High Intensity Use service, part of a national NHS programme of around 100, already collating data on exactly this cohort. What nobody is looking for in that data is ADHD — in the national HIU evaluation it appears only once, in a service user’s own words, in an appendix case study, never in a finding or a recommendation. A small report that goes looking is the natural first commission. The system is already paying — late, reactively and across the most expensive settings. The proposition is cost redirection, not new spending: drug and alcohol budgets already carry a heavy reactive load — crisis presentations, revolving-door admissions and repeat treatment episodes — and continuity is a cheaper place to spend. The published evidence supports the direction: roughly £17,000 in excess public-sector cost per adult with unsupported ADHD per year, from the independent ADHD Taskforce reporting to NHS England. That is Danish registry data compared against same-sex siblings, and it describes adults with ADHD rather than adults with ADHD and addiction — this cohort would plausibly cost more, which is an argument to test rather than a number to assert.

2 · Research funding

The research route runs through APPIE — the NIHR Policy Research Unit in Addictions’ national lived-experience research network, which invited me to join — and a growing bench of live academic contacts across eight universities, including the PI of an NIHR-funded waiting-list study, with follow-ups in motion. Early days, deliberately: the demonstrator produces exactly the local evidence a research bid needs and a research frame gives the demonstrator a credible evaluation. When the time comes to approach a Principal Investigator, OTOS arrives introduced, not cold.

3 · Licensing, once evidenced

Once the demonstrator has produced its evidence, the Layer itself becomes the product: voluntary-sector organisations and NHS services licensing a continuity layer that needs no access to clinical records, no systems integration and no new legal framework. That is what keeps it affordable for services with thin budgets — and why it can exist at all: everything that usually makes health technology slow and expensive has been designed out.

A quiet last word

This proposal is deliberately narrow — one cohort, one spearhead, one test. But a Continuity Layer has applications for other conditions and well beyond my own specialist area. Nothing in its design stops a person — and the system around them — keeping a Continuity thread for life.

The potential for OTOS Continuity infrastructure is huge, much bigger than this first use, but I am not running before I can walk.

Small print — boundary, disclaimers and NIA terminology

Boundary. OTOS does not diagnose, prescribe, treat, replace services or make clinical decisions. OTOS surfaces signals; partner teams make the decisions. No endorsement is assumed or implied — no organisation named on this page is a confirmed partner; services are named only as part of the founder’s own journey and any future role would be theirs to choose, only where agreed. No therapy content, notes or disclosures are part of OTOS; no NHS record access or write-back; no access to the NHS Single Patient Record is required. Any future work would be consent-led, partner-shaped and subject to governance and qualified clinical leadership.

Neurologically Informed Addiction (NIA) is founder-proposed terminology. It has not been submitted to any professional body and is not used elsewhere. It is a way of looking at ADHD-driven addiction through a different lens rather than grouping it with dual diagnosis or co-morbidity — because without a named problem there is no named treatment. There is live precedent for naming done properly: in May 2026, PCOS became PMOS — Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome — through a multistep global consensus process published in The Lancet, because the old name obscured what the condition actually is. ADHD-driven addiction deserves the same: a real, documented naming, arrived at properly.
