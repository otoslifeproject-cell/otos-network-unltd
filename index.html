When ADHD is left waiting, addiction steps in. And it doesn’t come quietly.

OTOS Continuity™ is non-clinical infrastructure that holds a person between the services they already use — so treatment, and the recovery work that follows, has time to land.

The problem

The system treats the crisis. Not always the condition driving it.

For a significant and identifiable group of adults, addiction is not the whole clinical picture. The visible crisis is alcohol, relapse, detox, A&E. The driver underneath is untreated or inadequately treated ADHD.

“ADHD, when unsupported, is a potent route into educational failure, long-term unemployment, crime, substance misuse, suicide, mental and physical illness. Economic costs to individuals and the government of at least £17 billion are avoidable as, when appropriately supported, people with ADHD can thrive and fully engage in a working life.”

Report of the independent ADHD Taskforce, NHS England, 2025

Self-medication works — until it doesn’t. Alcohol can briefly do what an untreated ADHD brain has been trying to do all along: cut through boredom, settle internal noise, create focus, bring a person into the room. That is not a moral failure. It is a neurological system finding something that, for a time, helps it function. And then it crosses the line into dependence, and compounds everything it once relieved.

Then the treatment itself misses. Recovery is a learning process — therapy, relapse prevention, recovery education, routine — and it asks for exactly the capacities unmanaged ADHD impairs: planning, working memory, impulse control, delayed reward. Fund recovery without stabilising the ADHD underneath it, and you are asking someone to succeed using the one system that has been disabled.

NHS England’s own ADHD data carries a warning about itself: it is “likely to significantly underrepresent the number of people seeking, waiting for and with a diagnosis of ADHD.”

The Royal College of Psychiatrists has been explicit about what should happen: “Significant and comorbid substance misuse should be managed jointly between general adult and addiction services, with both teams aware of treatment plans and able to support each other” (CR235). Its 2025 report on co-occurring conditions goes further — “assertive linkage to appropriate mental healthcare should be considered a core role for substance use services” (CR243). On the question every addiction service asks first, CR235 §11.1 is unambiguous: “when prescribed stimulants are used appropriately, there is little evidence of patients becoming dependent, and indeed treating ADHD may reduce the lifetime risk of developing a substance use disorder.”

The guidance exists. The connective tissue to deliver it does not.

02

The gap

Nobody owns the risk in the gap.

Every service owns its own episode. Nobody owns the time between them. It is not a gap in care — it is unowned risk.

The hospital owns the admission. The addiction service owns the treatment episode. The ADHD service owns the assessment. The GP owns the consultation. No one is accountable for the days, weeks and months in between — and that unowned time is exactly where this group is lost.

They do not disengage loudly. They drift, silently, at hand-off — after a detox, after a discharge, maybe signposted, but alone at the moment of highest risk. A referral is not proof that a connection happened. A discharge plan is not proof that the next step landed. A waiting list is not a holding layer.

And there is a reason it happens to this group in particular. A handoff is not an administrative event — it is a cognitive task handed to the patient: remember something said once, hold it across weeks, and start an action with no immediate reward, while unwell. For an unmedicated ADHD brain, that is the hardest possible ask. Reward is steeply discounted over time, so a date three weeks away never feels close. Working memory is leaky, and the instruction was verbal. Initiation — not motivation — is the barrier. Miss the first appointment and shame makes the second harder. Meanwhile the untreated condition keeps generating the state that alcohol reliably fixes.

Drift is not a choice. It is a predictable consequence of a known neurology — which means it can be designed for.

03

The venture

Infrastructure, not another service.

OTOS Continuity™ is a non-clinical continuity layer: one person, one continuity thread, running between the services they already use. Nothing changes in how those services work. What changes is that the gap is no longer empty.

Consolidation happens across days, not inside a weekly hour — and the gaps between appointments are exactly where support currently disappears. So the thread works those gaps on four jobs:

  1. Surface — micro check-ins and early drift signals, so risk becomes visible before it becomes crisis.
  2. Hold — prompts and transition receipts, so a handoff is something that can be confirmed rather than assumed.
  3. Reinforce — recovery-literacy repetition, education and digital mentoring between sessions, keeping the learning alive on the days no service is in the room.
  4. Evidence — consent-led signals, so the intervention can be tested, improved, continued or stopped on what actually happened.

Crucially, it needs no access to anyone’s clinical record. NHS England’s own framework on integrating voluntary-sector organisations names the barrier plainly: caution about breaching confidentiality or GDPR, and no legal framework to share data between the NHS and VCSE organisations. A consented, non-clinical continuity layer sidesteps that entirely — which is why it can be tested in months rather than waiting on a national programme.

What OTOS is

  • A non-clinical continuity layer around services that already exist
  • Consent-led, with the person in control of their own thread
  • Grounded in NICE NG87 and RCPsych CR235 / CR243 guidance
  • Built from first-hand experience of this exact user journey
  • Designed to be tested, and to be stopped if it does not work

What OTOS is not

  • A clinical service, diagnostic tool or treatment
  • An electronic patient record — no record access, no write-back
  • A replacement for any existing service
  • A claim of guaranteed savings — modelling is labelled as modelling
  • A general messaging platform for existing activity
04

The founder

I am not adjacent to this problem. I lived it.

I was diagnosed with ADHD in 2020. I was referred for an NHS medication review in March 2021 — and I am still on that waiting list. Five years, and counting.

For twenty-five years, alcohol did the work my untreated ADHD had left undone. Then it stopped working and started costing: six detoxes, two ICU admissions, A&E, courts, probation, residential rehab — where my ADHD medication was removed on arrival because it was a stimulant. Every service I met did its own job properly. None of them were connected, and none of them asked why I was the kind of patient who kept coming back.

In September 2025 I reached the right medication privately, funded by my parents. I felt the change within hours. The recovery work that had floated around me for years finally had somewhere to land — and in December my consultant psychiatrist wrote, in a clinical letter, that my alcohol dependence “was because of inadequately treated ADHD and a means to self-medicate.”

I did not recover by waiting my turn. I recovered by navigating a fragmented system fast enough to reach the intervention that changed my life. My story isn’t unique. My survival journey is.

ADHD is a disability under the Equality Act 2010. I am not an applicant who happens to have relevant lived experience — I am building the thing I needed and could not get, for the people who cannot do what I did. Many of them do not yet know they have ADHD at all. They are simply living the pattern I lived.

That experience is not the pitch. It is the design specification. I am now part of the APPIE network — the NIHR Policy Research Unit in Addictions’ national lived-experience research network — and I am working toward a formal research project on this seam, where lived-experience contributors are named as co-applicants rather than consulted afterwards.

The award

What the money does.

Twelve months, three stages, and a specific thing that exists at the end of each. This award does not build the product — it builds the case that makes the product fundable.

WhenWhat gets built
Months 1–3The evidence base: the local picture assembled and the research question defined, with lived-experience contributors involved from the first week rather than shown the result at the end.
Months 4–6The governance pack that makes a service able to say yes: data protection impact assessment, consent model, safeguarding protocol, information-governance review, and clinical safety documentation authored with a registered clinical safety officer.
Months 7–12Partner agreements, insurance and formation complete — and a costed, specified demonstrator that a commissioner or funder can be asked to fund.

What we will measure: how many handoffs are receipted · what proportion of participants reach the next step · how quickly someone re-engages after drift · what participants say about being held · and — asked openly — whether we add burden for the practitioners involved.

Sustainability

Three routes, and one of them is already open.

1 · Local-authority and NHS commissioning — live now

A public health commissioner in Cambridgeshire asked to see the full proposal following a meeting in April 2026. The proposition is cost redirection, not new spending: drug and alcohol budgets already carry a heavy reactive load — crisis presentations, revolving-door admissions, repeat treatment episodes — and continuity is a cheaper place to spend. The published evidence supports the direction: roughly £17,000 in excess public-sector cost per adult with unsupported ADHD per year, from the independent ADHD Taskforce reporting to NHS England.

2 · Research funding

The lived-experience research route opens NIHR and comparable funding, where contributors are named co-applicants. A demonstrator produces exactly the local evidence a research bid needs — and a research frame gives the demonstrator a credible evaluation.

3 · Licensing, once evidenced

Voluntary-sector organisations and NHS services paying for a continuity layer that needs no record access, no systems integration and no legal framework that doesn’t yet exist. Low integration is what makes it affordable — and what makes it possible at all.
